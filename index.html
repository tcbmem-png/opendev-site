§ 01 — The bet

The skilled trades have extraordinary people and almost no systems.

Every cleaning crew, painting crew, and drywall crew I've worked with in fifteen years of building things in Memphis has the same shape: the people are great, the back office is a wreck. No website. No real brand. No payment processing. No bilingual scheduling. Most go through a general contractor or a handful of word-of-mouth clients, and they keep maybe half of what their work is worth.

I want to fix that, for a few crews I know personally, and make a living doing it.

The shape is simple. I bring the brand, the website, the customer acquisition, the back-office systems, and the legal scaffolding. The crews bring the work — the people, the relationships, the craft. We split the revenue. They keep the lion's share. I take a percentage in exchange for the platform.

Each vertical is its own brand. Cleaning is one. Paint and drywall is another. Eventually a third and a fourth. Underneath, the same operating system runs all of them.

Most of these crews work for less than they're worth — not because they aren't excellent, but because nobody has built them a way to go direct.

§ 03 — Why me

Fifteen years of building in Memphis, and the strangest education for what comes next.

I started Yolo — Memphis's first frozen yogurt shop — in my late twenties, after a couple of years practicing tax and estate law. From there: Chiwawa, Tamp & Tap, Bedrock Foods, Memphis Contract Furniture, Bounty on Broad. Then operating partner with the Party Memphis group on Railgarten, Loflin Yard, Rec Room, Highland Axe & Rec, and the Tennessee Brewery Untapped activation that helped save the Tennessee Brewery.

The thread through all of that wasn't restaurants or hospitality. It was placemaking — building things in Memphis with the people of Memphis. That's still what I want to do.

The pivot was forced, and it changed the work

March 2020 closed all of it overnight. I had bartenders, cooks, and managers with no work, and a garage with some trucks. We started Two Broke Bartenders by delivering toilet paper and liquor. Then odd jobs. Then real general contracting. Five years later it's a real business that's employed dozens of people through the worst of it and steadily ever since.

I wrote at the time, in a column for the Daily Memphian: "Yesterday I scraped up someone's leaky trash bags from the side of the road and threw them into the back of a truck to haul off. It's hard to believe just two weeks ago I was planning parties, booking bands and editing menus." That moment, more than anything I've done in business, is what got me here. The crew that showed up in my garage that month is the same kind of crew I'm building this for now.

Two people I want to name first

Before I get to the law part, the most important thing in this arc happened the year Yolo opened. I met a man — call him M. — who has worked alongside me on every venture since 2010. The yogurt shop. The restaurants. The bars. The Airbnbs. The full-gut rehabs. The pandemic odd jobs. Fifteen years, side by side. His wife — call her A. — helped me run the cleaning side of the short-term rentals for years.

If this idea works, it's because two people decided to keep showing up alongside me through every kind of weather. They're the original proof of the thesis: the right people, paired with the right systems, can build something that lasts. I'd like both of them in real roles in this next chapter — him on construction, her on cleaning — and we're working through what that looks like.

Back into law, on different terms

I came at law from a different direction than most. Finished law school and a tax LLM in 2007, practiced for a couple of years, then went inactive in 2010 to start Yolo. The fifteen years that followed were the operator years — the restaurants, the bars, the placemaking, the pandemic, the garage. Last year I reactivated my license in Mississippi for immigration and small business work alongside the estate and tax work I'd done before.

Most of my practice now is immigration. Many of my clients are Spanish-speaking trades operators across the South. I've spent the last several years sitting across the table from cleaners, painters, drywallers, framers, roofers, and small contractors — listening to the same problems on repeat. Bad customers who don't pay. Worse contractors who clip them on the back end. Language and paperwork barriers that keep them subcontracting forever. Years of skill and almost no leverage.

I also managed fifty short-term rentals and ran my own cleaning team for two years, and I've done full-gut home rehabs alongside the same Memphis crew for four years. I'm not a tech person who learned about trades. I'm a trades operator and an attorney who learned to build software.

The systems part, finally

Over the last several months I've built the kind of operating systems most small businesses can't afford to commission. A working hospitality platform for Two Broke Bartenders. Operating dashboards for two small general contractors in the South. A full multi-market cleaning platform for Ladies Primera (more on that below). Each one taught me something about what's reusable and what isn't. The infrastructure is good now — good enough to start replicating, with discipline.

§ 05 — What's next

Paint and drywall, after cleaning is proven.

Once Ladies Primera has run a real cycle — real revenue, real settlement, real operators paid on real schedules — the next vertical is paint and drywall. Working name: Primero Paint + Drywall. Same two markets. Same model. Two crews already identified, already trusted.

Paint is a different business than cleaning. Higher ticket sizes. Project-based, not relationship-based. Real estimates, signed scopes, photos that protect everyone — the homeowner, the operator, and the brand.

Most of the platform code is reusable. Financial logic, scoping, scheduling, Telegram alerts, lead intake — one fork away from running a second vertical. The marginal cost of business #2 is much lower than the cost of #1. That's the whole point of building it the way I built it.

Then geography, before more verticals

Once both verticals run cleanly here, the next move is to expand each into a third and fourth market — Nashville, Atlanta, Birmingham, Jackson — before launching a third vertical. The brand becomes the product. New markets are the distribution.

Eventually a parent brand. Eventually one story that ties cleaning, paint, drywall, and whatever's next together. That's a five-to-fifteen-year build. I'm okay with that. I've spent fifteen years building things in this city that took longer than I thought they would. This one is meant to.

§ 06 — The bigger picture

Why this, and why now.

The trades aren't going anywhere. It'll be a long time before robots paint the inside of a house or scrub a tub. The work is human, local, immediate. What's broken isn't the work — it's the layer above the work. The brand, the systems, the customer acquisition, the back office.

Most people who do this work in the South — and especially in the immigrant communities I've spent the last several years working with through my law practice — don't have access to that layer. Building it for them, in partnership with them, is genuinely useful. It's also, I think, a real business.

I'm 45. My son is older now and finding his own way. I have less interest in chasing the next shiny thing and more interest in working with people I already love alongside, on something that compounds. The white-collar version of my career has been hard to feel excited about for a few years. This isn't a pivot away from law. It's a pivot toward the people my law practice has put me next to.

If this works, it lasts a long time and employs a lot of people fairly. If it doesn't, I'll have spent a year or two building something with friends I trust, learned a great deal, and returned to law a smarter operator. The downside is acceptable. The upside is large enough that I want to find out.

§ 07 — What I'm asking for

Tell me where this is wrong.

This isn't a pitch deck. I'm not raising money. I'm not selling anything.

What I want is honest reaction. If something rings false — a claim that's softer than I think, a market I'm wrong about, a person I'm overestimating, a risk I'm hand-waving — say so. If you've watched me long enough to know where I tend to drift, name it. If you know someone in the trades or the property world who'd want to hear about this, send them my way.

If you think it's right, I'd rather hear it on tougher terms. Where would I be in trouble first if this scaled? is a more useful question than do you think it'll work.

For a fuller picture of where my head's been the last few years, I write more personally at @tcbmem on Substack. Less business, more honest. If you've read this far, you've earned both.
